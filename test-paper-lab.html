Part 1 of 7 · Forces

What is a force?

Open a door, kick a ball, squeeze a tube of toothpaste. Every one of these needs a force. In science, a force is simply a push or a pull, and we measure how big it is in newtons (N).

Key pointsForce
  • A force is a push or a pull.
  • The S.I. unit of force is the newton (N).
  • The forces we meet every day fall into two groups:
    • Non-contact forces act across a gap, e.g. gravitational force and magnetic force.
    • Contact forces need the objects to touch, e.g. friction.

You can't see a force, but you can always see what it does. A force can set a still object moving, stop a moving one, change its speed or its direction, and squash or stretch it out of shape. Try each one below.

Forces also come in two families. Contact forces only act when objects touch: friction, a hand pushing a trolley, the air rubbing past a falling ball. Non-contact forces reach across empty space: the Earth pulls a ball down without touching it, and a magnet can tug a pin from a few centimetres away.

Measuring a force

To measure a force we use a spring balance. The harder you pull on its hook, the more its spring stretches, and the pointer moves along a scale marked in newtons. Beam balances and electronic balances are different: they are used to find mass, not force.

Always check the reading before anything is hung. Some balances don't start at zero. If the pointer already shows 10 N with nothing on the hook, the weight of an object is the change in the reading, not the final number.

Key takeaway
  • A force is a push or a pull, measured in newtons (N) with a spring balance.
  • A force can make a still object move, stop a moving object, change its speed or direction, and change an object's shape or size. It cannot change its mass.
  • Gravitational and magnetic forces are non-contact. Friction is a contact force.

Part 2 of 7 · Gravitational force

Why does everything fall?

Throw a ball as hard as you like and it always comes back down. The force responsible is the gravitational force, often just called gravity. The Earth pulls everything towards its centre, even while the ball is still flying upwards.

Key pointsGravitational force
  • Gravitational force (gravity) is what gives objects their weight.
  • The stronger the gravitational pull, the greater an object's weight.
  • The weight of an object on Earth can be found with: Weight = Mass × Gravitational force
  • The gravitational force of the Earth is 10 N/kg: every kilogram is pulled down with 10 N.

Watch the purple arrow: it never changes, and it always points down. On the way up, gravity acts against the motion, so the ball slows down. At the very top the ball stops for an instant. On the way down, gravity acts in the same direction as the motion, so the ball speeds up. Switch gravity off and nothing ever pulls the ball back.

Mass and weight are not the same thing

Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms (kg). Weight is the gravitational force acting on that mass, so it is a force, measured in newtons (N). On Earth each kilogram is pulled with 10 N, so a 50 kg student weighs 50 kg × 10 N/kg = 500 N. Take the same student to the Moon and the mass is still 50 kg, but the weaker pull makes the weight much smaller.

Every mass pulls every other mass

Gravity isn't something only the Earth does. Any two objects with mass attract each other: you and your desk, the Earth and the Moon, the Sun and every planet. The pull is stronger when the masses are bigger or closer together. Between everyday objects it is far too tiny to notice, which is why we only feel the Earth's pull.

This is also what keeps a satellite in orbit. The Earth's gravitational force pulls the satellite towards the Earth's centre all the time, and the satellite pulls back on the Earth just as hard. It is gravity, not magnetism, that holds the satellite on its path. Without that pull the satellite would not stay up: it would fly off in a straight line.

Key takeaway
  • Gravity gives objects weight: Weight = Mass × Gravitational force.
  • On Earth the gravitational force is 10 N/kg. Weight is in newtons (N); mass is in kilograms (kg).
  • Mass stays the same everywhere. Weight changes when the strength of gravity changes.
  • Every object with mass attracts every other object with mass. Bigger masses pull harder.

Part 3 of 7 · Magnetic force

Pushes and pulls without touching

Hold two magnets close and you can feel them tug or shove before they even touch. Magnetic force acts between magnetic substances such as iron, steel, nickel and cobalt, and like gravity it is a non-contact force.

Key pointsMagnetic force
  • Magnetic force is the force between magnetic substances.
  • The law of magnetism: like poles repel and unlike poles attract.

Every magnet has a north pole (N) and a south pole (S). Tap a magnet below to turn it around, then release them and see what happens when different poles face each other.

Key takeaway
  • Magnetic force acts between magnetic substances (iron, steel, nickel, cobalt) and works across a gap.
  • Like poles repel (push), unlike poles attract (pull).
  • Gravity acts between any two objects with mass, but magnetic force only acts on magnetic substances.

Part 4 of 7 · Friction

The force that slows things down

Give a ball a push across the floor and it rolls for a while, then stops. Nobody grabbed it. It was slowed by friction, a force that appears whenever two surfaces in contact rub against each other.

Key pointsFriction
  • Friction is a force that exists when two objects are in contact with each other.
  • Friction slows down the motion of an object.
  • Wheels, rollers and grease are ways to reduce friction.
  • Friction can be a nuisance: it causes wear and tear, and it turns useful energy into heat that can't be used any more.
  • Friction can be useful: it gives us grip, so we don't fall when we walk or run.

Friction always acts against the direction of motion. Rough surfaces grip each other more, so friction is larger. Push the same block across different surfaces with the same force and compare how far it goes.

On ice the block glides a long way because friction is small. On rough wood friction is large: the block stops after a short distance, and the rubbing surfaces warm up much faster. So to make a ball or block keep moving for longer, you can use a smoother surface, reduce friction with wheels, rollers or grease, or start it with a bigger push.

Friction from the air

Air rubs against anything moving through it. This air resistance is a kind of friction, so it always acts against the motion. Drop a ball from the top of a tall building and two forces act on it: F1, air resistance, pushing up, and F2, the gravitational force, pulling down.

As the ball speeds up, air resistance grows, so the speed rises more and more slowly until F1 balances F2 and the speed stops changing. F1 slows down the falling ball compared with a fall with no air. Every object falling towards the Earth feels F2 because the Earth's gravity pulls everything with mass towards it. Far out in deep space, away from the Earth's pull and with no air, neither force acts.

Key takeaway
  • Friction is a contact force that acts against motion and slows objects down.
  • Rougher surfaces give more friction. Wheels, rollers and grease reduce friction.
  • Friction wears things out and turns useful energy into heat, but it also gives us grip.
  • Air resistance is friction with the air. It acts upwards on a falling object.

Part 5 of 7 · Pressure

Same force, different pressure

Why does a sharp knife cut better than a blunt one, and why do your feet sink into sand while a beach mat doesn't? It's all about pressure: how much force pushes on each bit of area.

Key pointsPressure
  • Pressure is a force acting on an area.
  • The pressure exerted by an object is found with: Pressure = Force ÷ Area
  • The S.I. unit of pressure is the pascal (Pa), which is the same as N/m².

The same person, with the same weight, can press on the ground very differently depending on what's on their feet. Pick a shoe and watch how deep it sinks into soft sand.

The weight hasn't changed, only the area. A thin heel concentrates the whole force onto a tiny area, so the pressure is huge and it sinks. A snowshoe spreads the same force over a large area, so the pressure drops and you stay on top. Smaller area, bigger pressure.

Flip the block

      • Pressure = Force ÷ Area, measured in pascals (Pa) or N/m².

      Part 6 of 7 · Work done

      When a force moves something

      In everyday English, "work" means any effort. In science it has a sharper meaning. Work is done when a force makes an object move through a distance, and every time work is done, energy is transferred from one thing to another.

      Key pointsWork done
      • Work is a transfer of energy.
      • Work is done when a force causes an object to move a distance.
      • The work done on an object is found with: Work done = Force × Distance
      • The object must move in the same direction as the force applied on it.
      • The S.I. unit of work done is the joule (J).
      Try it

      Double the force or double the distance and the work done doubles, which is why both appear in the formula. Work done depends on the force applied and the distance moved.

      Force and movement must line up

      Work only counts when the object moves in the same direction as the force. Pushing hard against a wall that doesn't move does no work on it, however tired you get. Carrying books along a corridor is the tricky one: your hands push up to hold the books, but the books move sideways, so that upward force does no work on them.

      How big is one joule?

      One joule is the work done when a force of 1 newton moves an object through a distance of 1 metre in the direction of the force. A small apple weighs about 1 N, so lifting one apple up by 1 m takes about 1 J of work.

      Key takeaway
      • Work is a transfer of energy. Work done = Force × Distance, in joules (J).
      • Work is done only when the object moves, and moves in the same direction as the force.
      • 1 J = a force of 1 N moving an object through 1 m.
      • No movement (pushing a wall, holding a bag still) means no work done on the object.

      Part 7 of 7 · Energy

      Where energy comes from, and where it goes

      Every push, every lift and every spin of a turbine needs energy, and that energy has to come from somewhere. Here are the main sources of energy people use.

      Key pointsEnergy
      • Sources of energy include fossil fuels, solar energy, hydroelectric energy, wind energy, geothermal energy, biofuels and nuclear energy.
      • Conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only change from one form to another.

      Some sources are renewable: they are replaced naturally and will not run out, like sunlight and wind. Others are non-renewable: once used up, they take millions of years to form again, or never come back, like coal, oil and natural gas. Tap a source to follow its energy all the way to electricity.

      Explore

      Sources of energy

      Food fits this picture too. Plants use light energy from the Sun to make their food, and every animal in a food chain gets its energy, directly or indirectly, from plants. So solar energy is the main source of energy for plants, and for all living things.

      Energy is never lost, only changed

      The principle of conservation of energy says energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only change from one form to another. A roller coaster shows this clearly. Drag the cart along the track and watch the energy bars.

      With no friction, gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy simply trade places and the total stays at 200 kJ. Add friction and some energy becomes heat. It isn't destroyed: the three parts still add up to 200 kJ. But the heat spreads into the track and the air and can't push the cart, so the cart can no longer climb as high.

      What happens inside an appliance

      Electrical appliances are energy converters. Whatever goes in as electrical energy comes out as other forms: light, sound, kinetic energy and, almost always, some heat. Count the joules: what goes in always equals what comes out.

      Key takeaway
      • Sources of energy: fossil fuels, solar, hydroelectric, wind, geothermal, biofuels and nuclear.
      • Renewable sources are replaced naturally; non-renewable sources run out.
      • Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted from one form to another. Some usually ends up as heat.

      Common traps

      • !Weight is a force, mass is not. Weight is in newtons (N); mass is in kilograms (kg). A spring balance measures weight.
      • !Friction and air resistance are contact forces. Gravitational and magnetic forces are non-contact.
      • !A force can change speed, direction, shape or size, but never the mass of an object.
      • !Gravity is not only the Earth's. Every object with mass attracts every other; the masses decide how strong the pull is.
      • !Equal and opposite forces cancel. With 10 N one way and 20 N the other, the object moves in the direction of the 20 N force.
      • !No movement in the direction of the force means no work done. Holding or carrying something level does no work on it against gravity.
      • !Convert cm² to m² before calculating pressure in Pa. Divide by 10 000.
      • !Turbines convert energy; they don't store it. Food, fuels and water held high in a reservoir are stores of energy.

      The big picture
      Forces push and pull. Gravity gives weight, magnets attract and repel, friction slows things down, and pressure tells you how concentrated a force is. When a force moves something, work is done and energy is transferred, and that energy is never destroyed, only changed into other forms.
