9.1

How do forces in nature affect us?

The forces of nature built the world we live on. Volcanic eruptions piled up mountains, blasted out craters and left behind fertile soil. Over millions of years these forces carved out the landforms we see today.

The same forces can also destroy homes and take lives. Satellites now let scientists collect data that show the climate is changing quickly: the average global temperature is rising, polar ice caps are shrinking, sea levels are rising and extreme weather is getting more intense.

Nature also has its own shock absorbers. Coral reefs and mangrove forests sit between the open sea and the shore. They take energy out of incoming waves by reducing the height and speed of the water. Switch them on and off below and watch what reaches the buildings.

9.2

What are the types of forces?

Forces are what make walking, swimming, throwing and kicking possible. When engineers understand the different types of forces and how they combine, they can design planes that fly, ships that sail, cars that drive and rockets that launch.

Before a new plane is built, engineers test a model in a wind tunnel. A fan pushes air past the model, just like the air rushing past a real plane, and a balance measures the forces. There are four main forces on a plane in flight.

One goal of aircraft design is a high lift-to-drag ratio (L/D): plenty of lift for very little drag means the engines can work less hard, so the plane uses less fuel.

Contact and non-contact forces

Contact forces need the objects to physically touch. Examples are the normal force, tension, friction and the elastic force.

Non-contact forces act across a gap, even when the objects are not touching. Examples are the gravitational force, the magnetic force and the electric force.

Springs measure force

Stretch a spring with a load and it gets longer. The increase in length is called the extension. For a spring, the extension is directly proportional to the load: double the load and you double the extension. This is exactly why a spring balance can be used to measure force.

Drawing forces

A force has a size (magnitude) and a direction, so we draw it as an arrow. To draw the forces on an object:

  1. Find every force acting directly on the object (or on the point you are interested in).
  2. Draw each force as an arrow starting from where it acts, pointing in the direction it acts.
  3. Always label every arrow.

Key takeaway
  • Contact forces (normal, tension, friction, elastic) need touching. Non-contact forces (gravitational, magnetic, electric) act across a gap.
  • The extension of a spring is directly proportional to the load.
  • Draw every force as a labelled arrow from where it acts, pointing in its direction.
  • Mass (kg) never changes. Weight (N) = mass × gravitational field strength, so it depends on where you are.
9.3

What happens when two or more objects interact?

We cannot see forces, but we can see what they do. A force can change an object's motion, change its size or shape, or make it turn.

Glaciers: frozen rivers on the move Climate change A glacier creeps slowly downhill because of gravity. It changes speed very slowly, but its huge mass means it pushes with a great force. Friction between the ice and the bedrock opposes its motion, while meltwater underneath acts like a lubricant and reduces that friction. As the planet warms, glaciers are melting and slowly disappearing.

Turning effects of forces

Some forces make objects turn about a fixed point called the pivot (or fulcrum). The hinge of a door is a pivot: pull the handle and the door turns about its hinges. Simple machines called levers use turning effects, such as a claw hammer pulling out a nail, a wheelbarrow, and even your forearm.

Moments Extension

The turning effect of a force is called its moment. You can make it bigger by using a larger force, or by applying the force further from the pivot:

moment of a force = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot

The unit is the newton metre (N m). When the anticlockwise and clockwise moments are equal, the object is balanced. (In upper secondary Physics you will use moments to decide how stable objects are.)

Pressure

When a force is applied to an object, it creates pressure. How much pressure depends on two things: the size of the force and the area of contact. To stop a heavy object such as a cement mixer from sinking into soft ground, builders rest it on a wide board. A large area of contact reduces the pressure.

Factors affecting pressure Optional for N(A)/G2

Pressure is the force acting on a unit area. Its SI unit is the pascal (Pa).

Seismic dampers protect buildings Some buildings have seismic dampers fitted. When an earthquake moves the building, a piston moves inside a cylinder of silicone oil. Oil is pushed through a small hole from the chamber at higher pressure to the chamber at lower pressure. Friction between the oil and the hole opposes the force of the earthquake, so the building shakes less.

Atmospheric pressure

Atmospheric pressure is the force per unit area exerted on a surface by the weight of all the air above it. Imagine a column of air standing on 1 m² of ground, reaching all the way up through the atmosphere. Its mass is about 10 000 kg, so:

atmospheric pressure = force ÷ area = weight of air column ÷ area
= 10 000 kg × 10 N/kg ÷ 1 m² = 100 000 Pa (1 atm)

At sea level there are more air particles above you pushing down than at the top of a mountain, so atmospheric pressure is higher at sea level.

Liquid pressure

Water has weight too. The deeper you go, the more water there is above you, and the larger the gravitational force it exerts. So liquid pressure increases with depth. At the sea surface the pressure is 1 atmosphere (100 000 Pa), and it keeps rising as you dive deeper.

Calculating liquid pressure Extension

The pressure caused by a column of liquid can be calculated with:

pressure due to a liquid column = height of column × density of liquid × gravitational field strength

It depends only on the depth (height of liquid above) and the density. It does not depend on the shape of the container, the total mass of liquid or the surface area. This is why dams are built with a wide base: the pressure at the bottom of the dam is the greatest. Submarines are made of strong steel to withstand the high pressure deep in the sea, but even they have a limit to how deep they can go.

Science behind healthcare. Standing still for a long time lets blood collect in the legs, because blood pressure in the feet is higher than at the heart. This is why soldiers who stand still for long periods sometimes faint. Elastic bandages around the calves squeeze the legs and raise the pressure there, helping the veins push blood back up to the heart.
Key takeaway
  • A force can start, speed up, slow down, stop or turn a moving object, change its shape or size, or create pressure.
  • Balanced forces → no change in motion (still, or steady speed). Unbalanced forces → speeding up, slowing down or changing direction.
  • Moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot.
  • Pressure = force ÷ area. Atmospheric pressure falls with height; liquid pressure rises with depth.
9.4

What are energy and work?

Energy exists in many forms around us. When we understand how energy is transferred and converted, we can make better choices about how to generate electricity. Countries collect data on their energy sources so they can choose alternatives that suit their geography and environment, and play their part in using energy sustainably.

Energy Storage Systems (ESS) Emerging technology An ESS stores energy so it can be used later. Batteries store chemical potential energy. A flywheel stores kinetic energy in a heavy spinning rotor, and a generator converts it back to electrical energy when needed. Pumped hydro stores gravitational potential energy by pumping water uphill. Storage is essential for using solar energy, which comes and goes with the weather, while keeping the power grid stable.

Conservation of energy

Energy can be converted from one form to another. The law of conservation of energy says the total energy in a system stays constant: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted from one form to another.

In real life (a non-ideal system), some energy always escapes to the surroundings, usually as heat and sound:

KEi + GPEi + energy supplied to the system
= KEf + GPEf + energy lost to the surroundings
i = initial (start), f = final (end)

A swinging pendulum shows this beautifully. As the bob swings between A and C, energy changes back and forth between GPE and KE, but the total (GPE + KE) stays the same, as long as nothing takes energy away.

Work done

Work is done when a force moves an object through a distance in the same direction as the force. The SI unit of work is the joule (J), the same as energy, because doing work is how energy is transferred.

For work to be done by a force, two conditions must both be met:

  • the force is not zero, and
  • the object moves in the same direction as the force.

For example, when a driver steps on the accelerator, the engine pushes the car forward with force F and the car speeds up from 0 to v. The force has done work moving the car through a distance. Overall: chemical energy in the fuel → kinetic energy of the car. The energy transferred during the work equals the final KE minus the initial KE.

Key takeaway
  • Energy is the capacity to do work, measured in joules (J). Energy is converted, never created or destroyed.
  • In real systems some energy is always lost to the surroundings, mostly as heat (and sound).
  • Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force. No movement, or a force at 90° to the motion, means no work.
  • Power = work done ÷ time = energy ÷ time, measured in watts (W).
9.5

What are the sources of energy that we use?

Burning fossil fuels to generate energy and manufacture goods has increased greenhouse gas emissions, which trap heat in the atmosphere. Most of Singapore's electricity is generated from natural gas, one of the cleanest fossil fuels. But natural gas is non-renewable and will run out one day, so Singapore is working to scale up its use of alternative energy sources.

Should we cover the Sahara in solar panels? Climate change It has been suggested that a solar farm of 254 km × 254 km in the Sahara (which covers 9.2 million km²) could supply electricity to the whole world. But a 2018 climate-model study found a catch: covering 20% of the desert with dark solar panels, which absorb and give out more heat than sand, would warm the local area by about 1.5 °C. That warm air could spread and raise the global temperature by about 0.16 °C. Every energy source has trade-offs.

Renewable and non-renewable sources

We need energy sources to produce electricity for homes, factories, commercial buildings and vehicles. They include fossil fuels and alternative sources such as solar, hydroelectric, wind, geothermal, biomass and nuclear energy. They fall into two groups.

How power stations make electricity

Fossil fuels, geothermal energy, biofuels and nuclear energy can all produce electricity in the same basic way: the power station boils water into steam, the moving steam drives a turbine, and the turbine turns a generator that produces electricity. The only difference is the source of heat used to boil the water.

Some power stations use forces in nature (moving water or wind) to drive the turbine directly. Solar cells skip the turbine altogether. Explore each station and follow the energy.

Inside a nuclear reactor

Nuclear power stations release energy from the nucleus of an atom. When a neutron hits a uranium-235 nucleus, the nucleus splits (fission) into two lighter nuclei such as barium-141 and krypton-92, releasing a large amount of heat, gamma rays and three more neutrons. Those neutrons can split more uranium nuclei, causing a chain reaction.

The reaction is kept under control with control rods made of elements such as hafnium, boron, cadmium, silver and indium. They absorb neutrons without splitting. Lower the rods and see what happens.

Impact on the environment

Every source of energy affects the environment in some way:

  • Clearing land to build a power plant affects the livelihood of local communities and the ecosystem.
  • Hydroelectric dams can flood the area behind them, killing animals and plants.
  • Birds flying near wind turbines may be killed, and the turbines can be quite noisy.
  • By-products and toxic waste must be monitored closely, because leaks can cause water and air pollution.
  • Burning fossil fuels and biomass releases greenhouse gases and causes air pollution. More greenhouse gases contribute to global warming.
Key takeaway
  • Non-renewable sources (coal, oil, natural gas, nuclear) will run out. Renewable sources (solar, hydro, wind, geothermal, biomass) are replenished.
  • Most power stations boil water → steam turns a turbine → turbine turns a generator → electricity. Only the heat source differs.
  • Hydroelectric and wind stations drive the turbine directly. Solar PV cells convert light energy straight into electrical energy.
